A cardiologist from Brussels walks into a hackathon. It sounds like the opening to a joke, but in February 2026 it actually happened. Dr Michał Nedoszytko, an interventional cardiologist with no formal coding experience, built PostVisit.ai between hospital shifts and on the flight to San Francisco, and placed third at Anthropic's "Built with Opus 4.6" hackathon. A few years ago this would have been impossible. Today it is a signal of something much bigger: AI is no longer just answering our questions. It is starting to do things on our behalf.

In previous columns I looked at using large language models for referencing and then for statistical analysis. This piece takes the next step in that progression: from AI as a search tool, to AI as an analyst, to AI as a worker that can plan, use tools, and complete multi-step tasks without you hovering over every keystroke.

An AI agent is a system that can reason about a goal, break it into steps, call external tools, remember what it has already done, and keep going until the task is complete or until it asks you for help. The key difference from the chatbots we have all been using is autonomy. A chatbot waits for your next prompt. An agent takes an instruction and runs with it.

No story captures the excitement and the chaos of this moment better than OpenClaw: 100,000 GitHub stars within 48 hours, a remote code execution flaw, a skill marketplace where nearly a fifth of packages turned out to be malicious, and an agent that autonomously created a dating profile without its owner's knowledge. OpenClaw matters not because everyone should install it, but because it crystallised the core tension of agentic AI: these systems are genuinely capable and genuinely risky at the same time.

I have been experimenting myself, with a couple of self-hosted instances that monitor a research topic and a few data feeds and report to me over WhatsApp. The barrier to entry is genuinely low. But low barrier does not mean low risk. Run agents in sandboxed environments, use separate accounts and credentials, and do not give an agent access to anything you would not be comfortable losing control of. The moment delegation outpaces your oversight, you have a problem, not a productivity tool.

For clinicians and health researchers the harder question is not "can it do this?" but "should I let it?". An agent that organises your reference library is low stakes. An agent that drafts clinical correspondence or processes patient data is not. The difference is not technical capability; it is the consequences of getting it wrong. Regulators are drawing the same line: licensed clinicians remain responsible for AI-assisted decisions, and the WHO's guidance reinforces that AI should support, not replace, clinical judgement.

Build a healthy agent relationship: know what it can access, understand what it is doing, check its work, and scale your trust as you would with any new colleague. The cardiologist from Brussels did not trust his agent to practise medicine. He used it to build something that helps patients understand their care. That is the right instinct.
